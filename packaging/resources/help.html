FuseNest

Arranges flat parts onto sheet stock, then moves them there in your design — bodies and component instances alike, in the document you have open.

Steps

  1. Select the bodies and components to nest. Each one is reduced to the outline it casts on the XY plane, so parts must be flat and lying down.
  2. Set the sheet size and the spacing to keep between parts. Leave the width at zero to nest onto one unbounded strip instead of sheets.
  3. Choose how far parts may rotate. Fewer angles respect grain and finish direction; continuous packs tightest.
  4. Press Nest. The layout appears as a preview and keeps improving while the solver runs — press OK at any point to take the best layout so far, or Cancel to leave the design untouched.

Applying a nest moves real geometry. Anything holding a part in place — a joint, a rigid group, a grounded component — is released so the part can reach its spot, and FuseNest says so when it does. A single Undo puts back both the moves and the constraints.

Settings

Time budget
How long the solver may search. It is a ceiling, not a target: the preview updates continuously and OK takes whatever is on screen, so a long budget costs nothing if you stop early.
Workers
How many threads the solver uses. More threads explore more layouts per second; leaving one or two cores free keeps Fusion responsive.
Capture diagnostics
Records what the solver did, in detail, plus two files describing the outlines in the nest. Off by default. Turn it on when you are about to report a problem — see Privacy below for exactly what that writes down.

Privacy

FuseNest does not collect, transmit or store anything anywhere. It has no network code. Everything it does happens locally, inside Fusion, in the design you have open.

It does keep a local log, so that a problem you hit can be explained afterwards. The log lives beside the add-in's settings, in Library/Application Support/FuseNest on macOS and %LOCALAPPDATA%\FuseNest on Windows, and the last five sessions are kept. Nothing reads it but you.

With diagnostics off, the log holds one line per event: how many parts were selected, the sheet size and settings you chose, how long the solve took, how many parts moved, and the reason for anything FuseNest refused to do. Part and component names appear, because a message about a part is useless without them. No geometry is written.

With Capture diagnostics ticked, the same log also gets the solver's own running commentary and timings, and two files are written next to it: fusenest_instance.json, which contains the outline of every part in the nest, and fusenest_solution.json, which contains where each one ended up. Those outlines are the shape of your parts — the one thing here you might not want to hand to anyone. They are overwritten by the next nest and are never sent anywhere; attaching them to a bug report is your decision to make, not something FuseNest does for you.

Reporting a problem

Tick Capture diagnostics, do the thing that went wrong, and the dialog will show you where the log is. Message boxes link straight to it.
